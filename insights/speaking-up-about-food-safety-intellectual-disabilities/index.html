Helping Employees with Intellectual Disabilities Speak Up About Food Safety

I argue that teaching employees with intellectual disabilities how and when to report a food safety problem is as important as teaching the task itself.

Most food safety training teaches what to do. Far less of it teaches what to say when something goes wrong, such as a dropped utensil, a cooler that feels warm, a coworker who skips handwashing, or feeling sick before a shift. For employees with intellectual disabilities, this gap can matter a great deal, because reporting a problem may involve uncertainty about who to tell, how to say it, and whether speaking up is welcome.

I argue that reporting should be taught as a skill in its own right. That means naming specific situations, giving the employee a short and consistent phrase to use, and identifying exactly who to tell on each shift. Visual supports can help, such as a card that shows common problems alongside a picture of the person to notify.

Practice matters as much as instruction. Brief role plays during training let the employee rehearse reporting before it happens for real, and managers can reinforce the habit by thanking employees every time they raise a concern, even when it turns out to be minor. The goal is for reporting to feel routine rather than risky.

Illness reporting deserves special attention. Employees need to know which symptoms mean they should tell a manager before working, and they need confidence that doing so will not cost them their job. When speaking up is taught, practiced, and welcomed, the kitchen gains another set of eyes on safety.
