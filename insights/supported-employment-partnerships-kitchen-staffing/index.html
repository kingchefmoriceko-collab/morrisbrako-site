Supported Employment Partnerships Can Strengthen Kitchen Staffing

I argue that partnerships with supported employment programs give kitchens a dependable source of workers with disabilities who arrive with training support already in place.

Foodservice operators talk constantly about turnover and unfilled positions. At the same time, many people with disabilities who want to work in kitchens never get through the door. I argue that these two problems are connected, and that supported employment partnerships are one of the most practical ways to address both.

Supported employment programs and state vocational rehabilitation agencies exist to help people with disabilities find and keep jobs. Many provide job coaches who learn the role alongside the new employee, break tasks into steps, and gradually step back as the worker gains independence. For a kitchen manager, that means a new hire who arrives with structured training support already attached.

Food safety belongs at the center of these partnerships. A job coach who understands handwashing, cross contamination, and temperature checks can reinforce those habits during the first weeks, when routines are formed. Managers can help by sharing their food safety procedures in advance, walking the coach through each station, and agreeing on how progress will be checked.

These partnerships work best as long term relationships rather than one time placements. When an operation becomes known as a reliable employer, programs can refer candidates who are well matched to the work. I see this as workforce development that makes the kitchen more inclusive and, done well, safer.
