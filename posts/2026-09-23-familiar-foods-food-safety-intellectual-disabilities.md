---
title: "Using Familiar Foods to Teach Food Safety to Employees with Intellectual Disabilities"
date: 2026-09-23
summary: "I argue that food safety lessons built around foods and routines employees already know are easier for employees with intellectual disabilities to learn and remember."
---

![](images/headers/inclusion.svg)

Food safety training often relies on generic examples, such as a raw chicken breast, a tray of lettuce, or a cooler of unlabeled containers. For many learners that works well enough. For employees with intellectual disabilities, abstract or unfamiliar examples can make a rule harder to connect to the real work. I argue that training lands better when it starts from foods and routines the employee already knows.

Familiar food gives a rule something to attach to. If the menu features a particular rice dish, stew, or sandwich, use that item to teach how long it can sit out, where it is stored, and how it is labeled. If an employee already cooks a familiar dish outside of work, that knowledge can be a starting point for talking about handwashing or keeping raw and cooked foods apart. The safety concept stays the same, but the example feels concrete and meaningful.

The same principle applies to the steps themselves. Teach each task at the employee's actual station, with the actual containers, labels, and tools they will use on shift. Pictorial checklists that show real menu items, rather than stock images, help the routine carry over from training to daily work.

Respecting what employees already bring to the kitchen, including the foods and food traditions they know, is part of inclusive training. It treats the learner as someone with knowledge to build on, which is a far better foundation for safe habits than starting from nothing.
